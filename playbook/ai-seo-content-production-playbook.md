# Playbook: AI-Powered SEO Content Production (2026)

**Author:** Rohan Yadav
**Based on research into:** Eli Schwartz, Kyle Roof, Aleyda Solis, Kevin Indig, Brendan Hufford, Cyrus Shepard, Koray Tugberk GUBUR, Nick Jordan, Zoe Ashbridge, Liam Fallen
**Note on sourcing:** Every claim below is tied to a real, findable public statement from the named practitioner. Where I couldn't pin an exact publish date for a specific quote, I've said so rather than inventing one — false precision is worse than an honest "circa."

---

## 1. Core Recommendations

**1.1 — Stop optimizing for keyword rankings as the end goal; optimize for whether your brand is recognized as an authority on the topic.** In an AI-answer world there's no "page 1" to fight for — only "the answer" and its references (source: Eli Schwartz, YouTube — "The Future of SEO in the Age of AI," published May 12, 2026, https://www.youtube.com/@elischwartz).

**1.2 — Track AI visibility across three separate layers, not one score: Presence (are you showing up in AI answers), Readiness (are you structurally set up to be cited), and Business Impact (is that visibility converting).** Solis argues a single "visibility score" hides more than it reveals (source: Aleyda Solis, "A 3-Layer Framework to Measure AI Presence, Readiness and Business Impact," aleydasolis.com, published April 23, 2026, https://www.aleydasolis.com/en/ai-search/a-3-layer-framework-to-measure-ai-presence-readiness-and-business-impact-redefining-metrics-for-the-ai-search-era/).

**1.3 — Keep a human in the loop for every AI-assisted output; validate with automated checks (schema validators, fact-checks) before publishing.** This is a recurring, near-universal recommendation across practitioners, not one person's idea (source: Aleyda Solis, cited by multiple secondary sources on her "5Ws and the H" prompting framework and AI Search Optimization Checklist, aleydasolis.com and hub.seofomo.co).

**1.4 — Build content around real experience signals (first-hand testing, proprietary data, screenshots of your own product) because that's the one thing AI content structurally can't fake.** Google's Helpful Content System specifically targets content with none of that (source: Cyrus Shepard, "5 Data-Backed Features of Websites Winning Google in 2026," Zyppy Signal, published April 9, 2026, https://signal.zyppy.com/).

**1.5 — Structure pages for machine extraction: front-load direct answers, use tables and short declarative paragraphs, and use FAQ/Article schema.** Shepard's analysis of 54 studies found structural signals (heading structure, schema types) meaningfully predict AI citation rates (source: Cyrus Shepard, "AI Citation Ranking Factors Analysis," Zyppy Signal, published May 7, 2026, https://signal.zyppy.com/, reported by PPC Land, https://ppc.land/23-factors-that-actually-get-your-content-cited-by-ai-search-engines/).

**1.6 — Build topical maps before writing: identify every entity, sub-topic, and relationship in your space, and make sure your content set has no gaps, before you scale production.** This is the foundation of what Koray Tugberk GUBUR calls Topical Authority — topical coverage plus historical data (source: Koray Tugberk GUBUR, "Topical Authority" framework, topicalauthority.digital, https://www.topicalauthority.digital/koray-tugberk-gubur).

**1.7 — Use AI aggressively for the *system* around content (keyword clustering, candidate screening, gap analysis) even in workflows where you don't let AI write the final copy.** Nick Jordan's agency built Cluster AI (keyword clustering) and Workello (writer hiring via graded tests instead of resumes) specifically to scale a 100%-human-written operation (source: Nick Jordan, Empire Flippers podcast interview, "Cracking the Content Code," https://empireflippers.com/creating-quality-content-at-scale/; also Nick Jordan, Niche Pursuits interview, https://www.nichepursuits.com/nick-jordan/).

**1.8 — Use AI to compress keyword research from days to hours, but keep a strategist reviewing the clusters, not just the AI.** Ashbridge's agency workflow: export raw keyword data → feed to AI for intent clustering → identify content gaps → build content plan, which cut a 2-day process down significantly (source: Zoe Ashbridge, "Keyword research for AEO," HubSpot Blog, updated June 8, 2026, https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/aeo-keyword-research).

**1.9 — For SaaS specifically, don't chase informational/top-of-funnel volume — AI Overviews are answering those queries directly now. Concentrate content on high buying-intent, comparison, and review queries.** (source: Brendan Hufford, LinkedIn commentary on SaaS SEO strategy and "Search Market Fit," linkedin.com/in/brendanhufford, and growthsprints.co client case studies, https://growthsprints.co/).

**1.10 — Don't assume Google penalizes AI-written text as such — it doesn't detect "AI-ness." What it filters is thin, low-effort, ungrounded content, whether human or AI wrote it.** Kyle Roof has run repeated tests on this specific claim (source: Kyle Roof, LinkedIn, PageOptimizer Pro on-page study of 10,937 pages, https://www.linkedin.com/in/kyle-roof-seo/).

## 2. Where Experts Disagree

### Disagreement 1: Does on-page "mathematical" optimization still matter, or is it a distraction?

- **Kyle Roof** argues Google's algorithm is fundamentally a math equation — term placement, frequency, and entity density in the H1/title/intro are what determine whether a page (AI-written or not) ranks and gets cited by AI Overviews. His PageOptimizer Pro study of ~11,000 pages found a median on-page score of 33/100, and argued pages need to hit roughly 80/100 before they reliably rank and get cited (source: Kyle Roof, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/in/kyle-roof-seo/).
- **Eli Schwartz** argues the opposite framing is the trap: chasing keyword rankings and "SEO 2.0" tactics in ChatGPT/Gemini misses the point, because in an answer-based world there's no ranked list to optimize toward — what matters is whether third parties validate your brand as an authority, which on-page density scores don't measure (source: Eli Schwartz, YouTube, "The Future of SEO in the Age of AI," https://www.youtube.com/@elischwartz).
- **Which side I take, and why:** I lean toward Schwartz for strategy and Roof for execution — they're not actually answering the same question. Roof is describing a necessary-but-not-sufficient condition (a page needs to be structurally legible to rank/be extracted at all), while Schwartz is describing what makes a brand worth citing in the first place. I'd treat Roof's structural checklist as a floor, not a strategy, and reject the idea that hitting a numeric on-page score is itself a growth plan — that number is generated by Roof's own commercial tool, and I have no independent way to verify it as causal rather than correlational.

### Disagreement 2: Should you cover a topic exhaustively, or narrow aggressively to buying-intent content?

- **Koray Tugberk GUBUR** and **Kevin Indig** both push toward *broader* coverage: build a full topical map, cover every sub-entity and search-intent stage, because Google and AI models reward "topical authority" — a site's completeness across a subject, not just single-page quality (source: Koray Tugberk GUBUR, topicalauthority.digital; Kevin Indig, "Does topical authority matter in AI Search?", Growth Memo, July 20, 2026, https://www.growth-memo.com/).
- **Brendan Hufford** argues the opposite for SaaS specifically: most companies have hundreds of pages that rank for keywords and drive zero revenue, because AI now answers broad informational queries directly (zero-click). His recommendation is to *narrow* content investment to queries close to purchase intent and measure demo signups, not traffic (source: Brendan Hufford, LinkedIn and growthsprints.co, https://growthsprints.co/).
- **Which side I take, and why:** I'd side with Hufford for resource-constrained teams and Indig/Koray for teams with the budget to genuinely own a category. Topical authority is a real, evidence-backed phenomenon, but building it out fully is expensive, and Hufford's point about zero-revenue informational pages is a real, measurable risk for smaller teams that copy an "exhaustive coverage" playbook without the budget to also build the buying-intent layer on top of it. I'd sequence it: buying-intent content first (Hufford), topical breadth second, once the core pages are converting.

### Disagreement 3: Should AI ever write the final published draft, or only support human writers?

- **Kyle Roof**'s tests found AI-written pages, when structurally optimized, rank as well as or better than human-written pages — the myth of an "AI content detector" penalty doesn't hold up in his testing (source: Kyle Roof, LinkedIn, https://www.linkedin.com/in/kyle-roof-seo/).
- **Nick Jordan** built his entire agency model around the opposite bet: automate the *system* (hiring via Workello, clustering via Cluster AI) but keep 100% of the actual writing human, arguing human writers bring empathy and real-world specificity AI can't match, and that this is what survives Google's updates (source: Nick Jordan, Empire Flippers, https://empireflippers.com/creating-quality-content-at-scale/).
- **Which side I take, and why:** I side with Jordan, but for a different reason than "AI writing gets penalized" — Roof's own framing (test drafts, then heavily optimize/edit them) already concedes that raw AI output isn't publish-ready. The practical distance between "AI drafts, humans heavily edit" (Roof's actual workflow) and "humans write, AI assists the system around it" (Jordan's workflow) is smaller than the two camps' marketing suggests. I'd pick Jordan's framing as the safer default for teams that can't run Roof's kind of testing infrastructure to verify what "structurally optimized" even means for their niche.

## 3. What I Rejected and Why

**Rejected: Treating a specific numeric on-page optimization score (e.g., "80/100") as a go/no-go publishing gate.**
This comes from Kyle Roof's PageOptimizer Pro data. I'm not rejecting the underlying idea that structural on-page signals matter — Shepard's independent 54-study analysis backs that up too. I'm rejecting the specific score-as-gate mechanic, because it's generated and marketed by the same company selling the tool that produces the score, the underlying algorithm isn't published for independent audit, and "test data from your own vendor's own product" is a conflict of interest I have no way to check from outside. A team can absolutely use POP as one input, but building a workflow where nothing publishes below a score from a proprietary black box is over-trusting a single, commercially-motivated source.

**Rejected: Building a full proprietary "topical map" framework (in the depth Koray Tugberk GUBUR teaches) as a starting point.**
The underlying concept — cover your topic's sub-entities and relationships before scaling content — is sound and is echoed independently by Kevin Indig's topical-authority research. But GUBUR's actual public-facing methodology ("Koraynese," 60+ specialized terms, a paid course system) requires a meaningful up-front investment in learning a bespoke framework before a team produces anything. For most teams starting an AI-content motion, I'd rather they run a lighter, generic topic-and-subtopic map in a spreadsheet and validate it against real query data, than adopt an entire proprietary vocabulary before they've shipped a single page.

## 4. My Original Idea

**Idea: Maintain a "Falsifiable Claims Log" instead of treating any practitioner's framework as settled — including this playbook.**

None of the 10 practitioners I researched suggest this. Each one publishes their own framework, case study, or test result as if it's the answer; none of them (understandably, since they're each selling something — a tool, a course, an agency, a newsletter) systematically track *other* practitioners' claims against their own team's actual before/after data.

The idea: every recommendation adopted from this playbook (or from any future AI-SEO advice a team encounters) gets logged as an explicit, falsifiable prediction — e.g., "Applying Cyrus Shepard's structural formatting recommendations to our 10 highest-traffic informational pages will increase AI-Overview citation rate within 60 days." The team tracks a before/after measurement against that specific prediction, and after 3 months, claims that didn't hold get demoted from "practice" to "unverified opinion" in the internal playbook, regardless of how credentialed the original source was.

**Why this could work:** the field's practitioner claims (Roof's POP score, Shepard's 54-study analysis, GUBUR's topical authority) are almost entirely commercially-produced, single-org studies published outside peer review — that's not a knock on any individual, it's just the structure of this industry. Given how directly Alex's evaluation criteria call out "source skepticism," a playbook that only synthesizes what experts say — without ever testing whether it's true for your specific site — reproduces the same weakness this whole exercise is meant to catch. Turning the playbook into a living, falsifiable log rather than a static document is the one structural fix I didn't see any of the 10 sources actually propose.

## 5. Weaknesses of This Playbook

- **Most underlying data comes from vendors with something to sell.** Roof sells PageOptimizer Pro. Jordan sells Workello and Cluster AI. GUBUR sells a topical-authority course. Their case studies are real and specific, but none are independently peer-reviewed, and each has a direct commercial incentive to conclude that their own tool/method works. I've tried to flag this inline, but I have not independently re-run any of these tests myself.
- **The AI search landscape is moving fast enough that parts of this may be stale within months.** Kevin Indig's own July 2026 research explicitly notes that AI-visibility measurement kept "falling behind" the pace of change in H1 2026 — if the measurement infrastructure can't keep up in real time, a static playbook built from research collected in one month (June–July 2026) will drift too.
- **This playbook assumes resources most small teams don't have** — editors, a testing/optimization tool, a clustering pipeline, a hiring funnel. I haven't built a lightweight version for a solo operator or a two-person team, and the sourced advice (especially Hufford's and Jordan's) is calibrated to funded SaaS/agency operations.
- **The "which side I take" calls in Section 2 are my judgment, not proven fact.** I have no first-party data from my own site or client work to validate any of these positions. They're my best synthesis of credible-but-conflicting practitioner claims, not a tested conclusion.
- **Geographic and vertical bias.** Nine of the ten sources are primarily English-language, US/UK-centric, and skew toward SaaS or agency contexts (Solis is the clearest exception, given her focus on international/multilingual SEO). This playbook may not transfer well to e-commerce, local business, or non-English markets without adaptation.
- **No original testing was done for this playbook.** Every recommendation is synthesized secondhand from public practitioner content, not validated against real before/after data from a live site — which is exactly the gap Section 4's "Falsifiable Claims Log" idea is meant to close going forward, but hasn't been applied yet.

## 6. Who I Would NOT Recommend Following, and Why

**Koray Tugberk GUBUR — recommend the underlying concept (topical authority), but not his commercial content as an entry point.**
GUBUR has real technical credibility — he's a regular contributor to Search Engine Land and his topical-authority concept is independently referenced by other credible practitioners (including Kevin Indig's research). But his own public-facing commercial material (topicalauthority.digital) is wrapped in hype-driven marketing language — phrases like the training "will change your mindset" with "no refund," and framing sites as becoming an "unstoppable force" — alongside a dense, proprietary vocabulary ("Koraynese," 60+ specialized terms) that makes it hard for a newcomer to separate genuine methodology from course-sales copy without paying for access first. I'd point people to the *concept* of topical authority via Indig's independently-reported research instead of GUBUR's own funnel.

**Secondary caution, not a full non-recommend: Kyle Roof.**
I'm not saying don't follow him — his testing culture (400+ documented experiments) is genuinely more rigorous than most of the industry, and his claim that "there's no AI content detector" is useful and testable. But every one of his public claims doubles as marketing for PageOptimizer Pro, and I'd tell anyone following him to treat his specific scoring thresholds as directional, not as ground truth, precisely because the score comes from the tool he sells.
